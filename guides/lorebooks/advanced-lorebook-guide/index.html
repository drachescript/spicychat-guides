Before you start

This is an advanced guide. It assumes you already know what Lorebooks are, roughly how they interact with context memory, and how to make an entry. The thread is aimed at the annoying part that comes after that: entries that do not trigger reliably, keywords that feel either too broad or too specific, and Lorebooks that start eating too much context.

The one rule the original guide keeps coming back to: keywords only trigger when the keyword actually appears in the conversation — typed by the user or produced by the LLM.

The trigger rule: anchoring is not triggering

A keyword sitting in the persona, scenario or another Lorebook entry does not directly activate the matching entry. The keyword has to appear in the actual chat.

That distinction matters because the guide later talks about putting special terms in a character's persona. That is anchoring: you are encouraging the model to naturally use a term in conversation, where it can then become a real trigger. The persona itself is not firing the Lorebook.

Persona says:
Ilyra bears the Mark of the Deep on her hand.

Lorebook keyword:
mark of the deep*

Nothing triggers just because the persona contains the phrase.
But if Ilyra later mentions the Mark of the Deep in chat, the phrase is now in conversation and the entry can trigger.

A lot of apparently "broken" Lorebook behavior comes from mixing those two ideas together.

Entry length & token usage

The guide recommends splitting information across multiple small entries rather than building one huge entry. Its rough target is under about 100 tokens per entry when possible.

The reason is context pressure. The guide gives the example that a roughly 1,000-character entry can consume around 250 context-memory tokens when it activates. Whether the exact number varies by model/tokenizer, the practical point is the same: a large entry gets inserted all at once and leaves less room for conversation and other relevant lore.

  • Smaller entries can trigger more precisely.
  • Layered information can stack only when it is relevant.
  • One giant entry is less likely to shove useful conversation out of context.
  • If one entry keeps growing, that is usually a sign that it should become several narrower entries.

The original phrasing is a good mental model: Lorebooks are reference libraries, not encyclopedias.

Wildcards

A wildcard is an asterisk attached directly to a word. It lets one keyword catch variations without listing every spelling separately.

Suffix wildcard

lunara*

This can match lunara, lunara's and lunaras. The wildcard only covers characters attached to that same word; it does not cross a space.

Prefix wildcard

*book

This can match storybook, lorebook and notebook, but not story book. Prefix wildcards are valid, but the guide treats them as much riskier because they can catch words you never meant to target.

Do not use *word*. The guide specifically warns against wildcards on both sides. They become extremely greedy and are likely to cause constant accidental triggers.

In practice, suffix wildcards are the useful default. They work best when the root word is already distinctive.

Umbrella entries

Very broad keywords tend to fire too often. This gets especially messy when one world Lorebook is shared across several characters: using {{user}} or {{char}} as world-information triggers can inject information that is irrelevant to the current bot.

An umbrella entry solves this by using a term that is broad enough to introduce a system but still specific enough to avoid firing constantly.

Too broad:
magic

Better world-specific terms:
aether
ley-line

Umbrella entry:
Faction: Adventurer's Guild
Keywords: adventurer*, adventurer's guild*

Content:
briefly establish that adventurers exist, belong to a guild,
and use a ranked system.

The umbrella does not need to explain every rank. It only teaches the model the structure and vocabulary. Copper, Iron, Steel, Mithril, Adamantine and Aetherium can each have their own entry later.

The goal is to steer the model toward the more precise vocabulary without permanently dumping all of the detailed rules into context.

Anchoring keywords

Anchoring means deliberately giving an important concept a distinctive term and placing that term in the character information so the model is more likely to say it naturally.

The guide's example is Ilyra's curse. A keyword like curse is far too generic, so the curse gets a proper name: Mark of the Deep. The persona establishes that the mark is a defining feature, and the Lorebook listens for mark of the deep*.

Anchoring improves the odds; it does not guarantee exact wording. You still have to watch what the model actually says.

Original keyword:
mithril rank

Model keeps saying:
mithril-ranked

Fix:
add mithril-rank*

This is why the guide describes anchoring as iterative. Do not guess forever at every possible variation. Test the bot, see which phrasing it naturally prefers, and add the useful variation.

Layering keywords

Layering combines the previous ideas: a broad-but-controlled entry establishes a system, then more specific entries activate as the conversation narrows.

Umbrella
Faction: Adventurer's Guild
Keywords: adventurer*, adventurer's guild*
→ defines the guild and says a ranked system exists

Specific entries
Guild Rank: Copper
Keywords: copper-rank*, copper rank*

Guild Rank: Iron
Guild Rank: Steel
Guild Rank: Mithril
Guild Rank: Adamantine
...

→ each rank explains its own expectations, power level and role

If a character persona says they are a Mithril-ranked adventurer, you are now using both systems together: the umbrella establishes the guild vocabulary, while the anchored rank makes it more likely that the specific Mithril entry will eventually trigger.

The result is that the model receives detail only when the conversation needs it instead of carrying the whole hierarchy in every response.

Broad entries vs specific entries

Broad keywords are not forbidden. The guide gives customised concepts such as guns, technology or implants as cases where a common term may genuinely mean something special in your setting. The rule is that the broader the trigger, the smaller and more factual the entry should be.

  • Broad entry: establishes context or a rule of the setting.
  • Specific entry: explains mechanics, behavior, consequences, authority or detailed expectations.

For the Adventurer's Guild example, the general adventurer* entry should say that ranks exist and list them. It should not also explain the power, privileges and expectations of every single rank. Those details belong in the rank entries.

How to write the entry itself

The thread recommends factual, compact, instructional writing. Lorebook text does not need to read like a novel.

Too prose-heavy:
Noctyrr is a continent governed by the undead entity Morvethra
where the corruption of undeath has overtaken the land...

Cleaner:
Noctyrr: continent, ruled by Morvethra, overrun by undead corruption.

Clear labels also make a large Lorebook much easier to maintain. The entry title is for you; it is not itself a trigger. The guide uses labels such as:

Faction: Adventurer's Guild
Faction: Philoponus Empire
Faction: Imperial Concord of Maritime Commerce
Character: Lunara Sylviel
Character: Goddess K'thara
Relic: Aeterna Luma

Later Q&A in the thread gives a similarly compact pattern for character entries:

char: Ursula
personality: sassy, chaotic, evil
goals: gain control of Falkenreach
description: villainous vampire lord; works from behind the scenes;
powerful vampire trying to regain control of a prominent city

The point is not that this exact schema is mandatory. It is an example of keeping the information the model needs without padding the entry.

Testing & maintenance

  • Actively test the words your bot uses instead of assuming your keyword list is correct.
  • If an entry misses, adjust to the model's natural phrasing rather than adding dozens of broad synonyms.
  • Build Lorebook entries alongside the character when possible; it makes useful anchors easier to spot.
  • Aim for roughly one or two relevant Lorebook activations per message rather than trying to trigger everything at once.
  • Use broader keywords only when the setting really gives that word a special meaning, and keep the entry tiny.
  • Guide the model with rules and boundaries rather than using Lorebook text to dictate every plot outcome.
